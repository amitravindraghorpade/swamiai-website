Pothole Detection Using CCTV: How Cities Can Find Road Damage Early

For a municipal engineering department, a pothole is rarely a surprise. The surprise is finding out about it from a newspaper photo, a viral video or a corporator's phone call. Many of the roads where potholes form are already covered by CCTV cameras. AI video analytics can turn those cameras into a daily road inspection that never gets tired.

The problem engineers know well

Ask any ward engineer or junior engineer about potholes and the same points come up:

  • Road damage grows fast in the monsoon, and a small crack becomes a deep pothole within days.
  • Complaints come in late and from many channels: helplines, apps, social media, letters and elected representatives.
  • Site inspections depend on staff time and vehicles, so busy roads get checked more often than inner roads.
  • When a repair is done, there is often no simple photo record of when the damage was first seen and when it was fixed.
  • During review meetings, it is hard to show which stretches keep failing and need resurfacing rather than patchwork.

Meanwhile, city and Safe City CCTV cameras record those same roads around the clock. The footage is there, but nobody can watch it all for road damage.

How AI pothole detection on existing CCTV works

An AI model is trained to recognise potholes, broken patches and other visible surface damage in camera images. It runs on the feeds of cameras the city already has, so a start does not require new cameras. The flow is simple:

  1. Select the cameras: choose cameras that have a clear view of the road surface, such as those at junctions, main roads and flyover approaches.
  2. Scan regularly: the AI checks the road area in each feed at set intervals, including in the early morning when traffic is light and the surface is most visible.
  3. Flag damage: when a pothole or damaged patch appears, the system records a snapshot, the camera location, date and time.
  4. Create a ticket: each detection becomes an incident with the ward, road name and severity filled in, so it is not lost in a WhatsApp group.
  5. Alert the engineering team: the responsible ward engineer or contractor gets the alert on a phone or dashboard.
  6. Confirm the repair: after patching, the same camera shows the repaired surface, and the ticket is closed with before-and-after photos.

The record of repeat damage is useful beyond daily repairs. It can support decisions on which roads need resurfacing, drainage work or a review of the contractor's defect liability period.

Being realistic about what it can see

A CCTV camera covers only the stretch of road in its view, and many city cameras point at traffic rather than the road surface. Night lighting, heavy rain, parked vehicles and water-filled potholes can all hide damage. That is why AI detection works best as an addition to field inspection and citizen complaints, not as a full replacement. The pilot stage is where a city finds out which of its cameras give a useful view.

Common questions from engineers

Do we need new cameras?

No. The system runs on existing CCTV feeds. Where an important road has no suitable camera, the city can decide later whether to add one or rely on other inspection methods.

Will it disturb our Safe City or police CCTV setup?

It should not. Road damage is a civic use case that is usually outside the scope of police analytics, so AI Smart Ward works alongside the existing system. Access to feeds is arranged with the agency that runs the cameras.

What about privacy?

The model looks at the road surface, not at people. Facial recognition is off by default.

Can it tell how deep a pothole is?

A normal CCTV camera cannot measure depth accurately. The system flags visible damage and gives it a rough severity based on size and appearance, so the field engineer can decide on the repair after a site check.

How to start: a small pilot

A sensible first step is a pilot on a limited number of cameras:

  1. Pick one or two wards and a set of cameras that clearly show busy road stretches with a known history of potholes.
  2. Agree who receives the alerts, such as the ward engineer and the road maintenance contractor.
  3. Run detection for a few weeks, ideally including some rainy days.
  4. Compare the AI alerts with complaints and field reports for the same stretches.
  5. Decide on expansion only after reviewing the results with your team.

The same platform can later cover garbage dumping, water logging, fallen trees and open manholes, each routed to the department responsible.
